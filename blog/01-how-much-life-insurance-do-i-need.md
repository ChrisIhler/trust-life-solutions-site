# How Much Life Insurance Do I Really Need? The DIME Method Explained.

*By Chris Ihler, Licensed Insurance Advisor · Trust Life Solutions*

---

Most people shopping for life insurance walk into the process armed with a single rule of thumb: **"buy 10 times your income."**

It's not wrong, exactly. It's just not right either. It's a back-of-napkin number that was useful 40 years ago when families looked more alike, mortgages were smaller, and two-income households were the exception. Today, applying "10x income" to every situation produces two bad outcomes at roughly equal rates: people end up underinsured because it didn't account for their real debts, or overinsured because it ignored the assets they already have.

There's a better way. It's called the DIME method, and the whole industry knows it — they just don't always explain it to clients because it takes longer than reading a number off a chart. This post explains it in full, walks through a real example, and shows you how to think about the result.

## Why "10x income" isn't enough

Start with what the rule actually does. If you earn $90,000 per year and your family depends on that income, $900,000 in coverage sounds like it replaces about ten years of earnings. Inflation-adjusted, it's actually closer to seven years of purchasing power. And it ignores every other financial obligation in your life.

Consider two families with identical incomes:

**Family A:** $90,000 income, renters, no kids, both spouses working full-time, $15,000 in student loans.

**Family B:** $90,000 income, $450,000 mortgage, three kids under 10, one spouse at home full-time, $35,000 in debt including a car loan.

Both get quoted the same $900,000 policy by the "10x rule." But Family A is almost certainly overinsured — the surviving spouse can cover their modest expenses from two incomes minus one. Family B is catastrophically underinsured — the coverage doesn't even pay off the mortgage, let alone replace 15 years of income plus fund three kids' college.

The same rule produces the wrong answer for both families. That's the problem with rules of thumb: they treat different situations as if they were the same.

## The DIME method

DIME is the framework most independent advisors actually use when building a real recommendation. It stands for:

- **D**ebt (everything except the mortgage)
- **I**ncome (the years your family needs support)
- **M**ortgage (remaining balance on your home)
- **E**ducation (what you want to fund for each child)

Each of those categories represents a specific financial responsibility your income currently supports. If your income disappears, each of those has to be covered — either by your family's existing assets or by the proceeds from a life insurance policy.

Here's how each category works in practice.

### D — Debt

This is every dollar you owe that isn't your mortgage: credit card balances, auto loans, student loans, medical debt, personal loans, and anything your cosigner or co-borrower would be stuck with.

Why not include the mortgage here? Because the mortgage gets its own category — it's so large and so different from other debts that treating it separately makes the math clearer.

Some debts disappear on death (most federal student loans are forgiven). Most don't. Credit cards, auto loans, medical bills, and private student loans persist and often get pulled from the estate before heirs see anything.

Add up every non-mortgage debt you carry. That's your D.

### I — Income

This is the most important number in the calculation, and also the most nuanced.

The basic version: multiply your annual income by the number of years your family would need that income replaced. If you earn $85,000 and your youngest child is eight, you might say you want to replace income for 15 years — until that child is out of college. That's $85,000 × 15 = $1,275,000.

The nuanced version: your family doesn't actually need to replace your full gross income. Some of your income gets spent on you personally (transportation, food, insurance for yourself) and disappears along with you. On the other hand, your family may need to *replace* unpaid labor you contribute (childcare, home management, commuting help) that gets outsourced to paid providers.

For most families, the gross-income-times-years number is a reasonable proxy. If you want to refine it, subtract 15–20% for your personal consumption and add back the annual cost of services your spouse would need to hire.

There's also the Social Security question. If you're a working parent, your surviving spouse and minor children may qualify for Survivor Benefits — typically 75% of your primary insurance amount, per eligible child. This can reduce the amount of coverage needed, particularly for families with younger kids. For most middle-income earners, Survivor Benefits cover $1,500–$2,500 per month per child, which adds up over 18 years.

For simplicity, the DIME calculation uses gross income × years. Refinement can come later in the conversation.

### M — Mortgage

This is the remaining balance on your home loan. Not the sticker price of the house, not what you bought it for — the current balance.

You want enough coverage that your family could pay this off in one shot if you died. The goal isn't that they should pay it off — they might choose to keep the mortgage and invest the proceeds instead. But having the option is what matters. Without it, they might be forced to sell the home in a hurry.

For most homeowners, this is the single largest line item in the calculation. If you have a $400,000 mortgage, it's nearly half a million dollars of coverage just to cover this one obligation.

### E — Education

The final category is education for any children you want to fund. The sticker prices are sobering:

- **Public in-state, 4-year degree:** ~$100,000 today, projected higher by the time current 5-year-olds enroll
- **Public out-of-state or private lower-tier:** ~$150,000–$180,000
- **Private top-tier (Ivy, Stanford, etc.):** ~$280,000+

Multiply by the number of children you want to fund. If you have two kids and want public in-state coverage for each, that's $200,000 in the education category.

You might choose to fund less than full tuition — many parents plan for 50–75% and expect kids to contribute via scholarships, work, or loans. Adjust accordingly.

## The adjustment: your existing assets and coverage

Raw DIME produces a ceiling, not a target. To get to a realistic coverage need, you subtract two things:

1. **Existing life insurance** — any policy you already have (personal or through work), up to the portion that would actually stay with you or be portable
2. **Available savings and investments** — liquid assets your family could draw on: brokerage accounts, savings, retirement accounts your spouse could access (though early withdrawal penalties apply for tax-advantaged accounts)

Raw DIME − existing coverage − available assets = your actual life insurance need.

This is where "10x income" goes wrong. It doesn't subtract anything. Family A in the earlier example might end up with $900,000 in recommended coverage when their true need — after accounting for the working spouse's income and existing assets — is closer to $250,000.

## A full walkthrough

Meet Sarah. She's 38, married to Mark, with two kids ages 7 and 10. She earns $95,000 as a software engineer. Mark earns $65,000 as a teacher. They own a home with a $325,000 mortgage. They have $28,000 in non-mortgage debt (student loans and a car loan). They have $180,000 in combined retirement accounts, $40,000 in a brokerage account, and $20,000 in emergency savings. Sarah has $200,000 in group life through her employer.

Running DIME on Sarah:

- **D** — $28,000 in debt
- **I** — $95,000 × 15 years = $1,425,000 (to cover until youngest is out of college)
- **M** — $325,000 mortgage
- **E** — 2 kids × $100,000 education goal = $200,000
- **Final expenses** (small add-on): $20,000 for funeral and estate settlement

Total obligations: $1,998,000

Offsets:
- Group life through work: $200,000
- Brokerage account (accessible): $40,000
- Emergency savings: $20,000
- Retirement accounts are accessible but with penalty; conservatively include half: $90,000

Total offsets: $350,000

**Sarah's actual life insurance need: $1,998,000 − $350,000 = ~$1,650,000**

Note that "10x income" would have suggested $950,000 — about 60% of what Sarah actually needs. The rule of thumb would leave her family a meaningful funding gap.

At Sarah's age and health (assume excellent, nonsmoker), a $1.5M or $1.75M, 20-year term life policy would run approximately $55–$85/month. Well within reach, and vastly better than being underinsured by nearly $700,000.

## The limits of DIME

DIME is a solid starting framework, but it's not perfect. It doesn't account for:

- **Inflation** over a 20-year term. $1.5M in 2045 dollars is worth considerably less than today.
- **Investment returns** on a lump-sum payout. Your family doesn't spend the whole $1.5M in year one — they invest it and draw from it. Conservative 5% growth on a $1.5M payout yields $75,000/year indefinitely without touching principal.
- **Stay-at-home parent contributions.** If a stay-at-home parent dies, the surviving parent now pays for childcare, housekeeping, and home management. DIME typically understates this at roughly zero.
- **Business ownership.** Business owners need key-person, buy-sell, and executive bonus considerations that DIME doesn't touch.
- **Tax considerations.** Large policies can trigger estate tax considerations. Complex, but relevant for high net worth.

For most families, DIME gets you within 20% of the right answer, which is much better than any rule of thumb can do. For complex situations, a conversation with an advisor adds the nuance.

## The real point

Most families are either significantly underinsured or significantly overinsured — rarely accidentally right. The difference comes from taking 15 minutes to run DIME instead of 15 seconds to apply "10x income."
