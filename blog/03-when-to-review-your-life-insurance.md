# When to Review Your Life Insurance: 7 Life Events That Change Everything

*By Chris Ihler, Licensed Insurance Advisor · Trust Life Solutions*

---

Most people buy life insurance once, put it in a filing cabinet, and forget about it. That's a mistake. The policy you chose at age 28 with a spouse and a starter home probably doesn't fit at age 38 with three kids and a move to a bigger house. And the policy you signed at 45 with a growing business probably doesn't fit at 55 when your kids are grown and retirement is on the horizon.

The good news: you don't need to review your life insurance on a schedule. You need to review it at **life events** — specific moments when your financial obligations, assets, or priorities meaningfully change. When one of these seven events happens, your insurance plan needs a look.

## 1. You got married (or entered a long-term partnership)

Most people know marriage changes their tax situation. Fewer realize it changes their insurance picture dramatically.

Before marriage, your life insurance need was probably small — enough to cover final expenses and maybe leave something to parents or siblings. After marriage, a spouse is depending on you (and you on them). Two things to do:

**Reassess coverage amount.** Use the DIME method to recalculate your need. Even if your spouse has their own income, the surviving spouse typically needs several years of your income replaced to adjust to a new financial life after a loss.

**Update beneficiaries.** If your current policy has your parents or siblings as beneficiaries, you almost certainly want to change that to your spouse. This takes five minutes by phone with your insurance carrier and can prevent genuine complications if something happens.

**Coordinate with your spouse.** If one of you has life insurance through work and the other doesn't, decide together who needs individual coverage and how much. A coordinated plan costs less than two uncoordinated ones.

## 2. A baby arrives (or adoption finalizes)

This is the most common trigger for first-time life insurance purchase — and for the biggest coverage shortfall among people who already have policies.

A baby adds 18–25 years of financial responsibility to your life. Food, clothing, childcare, healthcare, education, activities, college. Even by conservative estimates, the cost of raising a child to 18 is $200,000+. Add college and you're at $300,000–$500,000 per child.

When a baby arrives:

**Add coverage sized to the additional financial obligation.** For most parents, this means stepping up from a $250,000 or $500,000 policy to a $1,000,000 or $1,500,000 policy. The monthly premium difference is often $15–$40 — trivial relative to the expanded need.

**Consider a 20- or 30-year term.** A 20-year term covers your child from birth through college. A 30-year term covers them to independence and beyond. If you're buying at 32 and having your second kid, a 30-year term at locked-in rates is typically the right choice.

**Update beneficiaries and set up guardianship.** Your policy beneficiary list should name your spouse as primary and potentially your kids as contingent. Talk to an estate attorney about establishing a trust to receive proceeds if you and your spouse both die — you don't want the court deciding who manages $1.5M for your minor children.

## 3. You bought a home

If you just took on a $400,000 mortgage, you just took on $400,000 of financial obligation that doesn't disappear if something happens to you. The bank will expect to be paid whether you're alive or not.

When you buy a home:

**Add mortgage protection.** This is term life insurance sized to your mortgage balance and term length. If you have a $400,000 mortgage with 25 years remaining, you buy a $400,000, 25-year term policy. If you die, the policy pays off the house. Your family keeps it, free and clear. For a healthy 35-year-old, this costs about $28–$40/month.

**Don't confuse mortgage protection with "mortgage insurance" from the bank.** Those are different products. Mortgage insurance (MI or PMI) protects the bank if you default — it doesn't pay off your mortgage if you die. Mortgage protection is a term life policy that pays your family a death benefit, and they decide what to do with it (usually: pay off the mortgage).

**Don't use bank-sold decreasing term policies** if you can avoid them. Many banks offer "mortgage life insurance" where the payout decreases as your mortgage balance decreases. Those often cost more per dollar of coverage than a standard term policy of the same face amount. Go independent.

## 4. Your income jumped significantly

A promotion, a bonus, a business takeoff, inheriting a business — any event that meaningfully raises your income changes the equation in two directions.

**Your family now depends on a higher income replacement.** If you go from $90,000 to $140,000 in income, the amount your family needs to replace if you die just increased by 55%. A policy sized for the old income is now underinsured.

**Your estate tax exposure may have changed.** High earners and business owners can build estates that eventually face estate tax at death. Life insurance is one of the primary tools to fund estate taxes without forcing heirs to sell assets. If your net worth is climbing toward $5M+, this conversation becomes important.

When your income jumps materially (30%+ increase), review:

- Income replacement amount (typically years × new income)
- Whether your current term still has enough years left, or whether a new longer-term policy makes sense
- Whether permanent coverage (whole life, IUL) should play a role for estate planning or supplemental tax-advantaged growth

## 5. You started a business (or took on partners)

Entrepreneurship changes everything about your insurance picture. Three new categories of need appear:

**Key-person insurance.** If the business depends on you to function, and your absence would disrupt revenue for 6+ months, your business should carry a policy on your life. The business is the owner, payer, and beneficiary. If you die, the business has cash to stabilize, hire interim leadership, or wind down cleanly. Premiums are deductible as a business expense; payout is tax-free to the business.

**Buy-sell funding.** If you have a business partner, what happens to their stake if they die? Without a funded buy-sell agreement, you may inherit their spouse or kids as your new co-owners. A buy-sell funded by life insurance means each partner owns a policy on the other. When one dies, the policy pays the surviving partner enough to buy out the deceased partner's shares from their estate. The family gets cash; you get sole ownership.

**Executive bonus plans (if you have key employees).** A tax-efficient retention tool where the business pays the premium on a personal life insurance policy owned by the employee. You pre-tax your compensation, they get a portable policy they can't easily walk away from, and your retention improves.

Any of these apply? Get quoted. These policies typically pay significantly higher first-year premiums than retail term — don't overbuy, but also don't underinvest in protection for the thing that feeds your family.

## 6. A family member developed a significant health condition (or you did)

Two scenarios here. If you develop a health condition: your ability to buy new life insurance just got more expensive, and depending on the condition, might be impossible. If a family member develops a condition that increases your family's financial exposure (special-needs care, long-term care), your coverage needs changed.

**If you developed the condition:**

Before anything else, check if your current policy has a **conversion option**. Many term policies allow you to convert some or all of the coverage to permanent insurance — without new underwriting — up to a certain age (usually 65–70). This is one of the most valuable and least-understood features in the insurance world. If you're developing health issues and you have a term policy, check your conversion deadline immediately. Missing it costs you the right forever.

**If a family member developed the condition:**

Families with a child or spouse who will need long-term care (adult disability, serious special needs) often need permanent life insurance on the caregiver to fund lifetime care for the dependent. This is a case where whole life or guaranteed universal life can be the right product — not because the caregiver wants an investment, but because they need coverage that definitely pays out no matter when they die.

An independent advisor can structure this through special-needs planning, in coordination with an estate attorney who handles the trust structure.

## 7. You're approaching or in retirement

Most people need less life insurance at retirement than they did at 35 — your kids are grown, the mortgage is paid or nearly so, your spouse has their own retirement accounts. But the picture isn't zero.

**Final expenses still need coverage.** Funeral and burial costs average $10,000–$15,000. Estate settlement adds another few thousand. Unless your estate has liquid cash for this, a small whole life policy ($15,000–$25,000) makes sense specifically for this purpose.

**Term policies are approaching expiration.** If you bought a 20-year term at 40 and you're now 58, your coverage ends in 2 years. Options:
- Let it expire (if your family no longer needs it)
- Convert it to permanent before the deadline (if you still need coverage and health has declined)
- Replace with new term (if you're still healthy enough to qualify for reasonable rates)

**Long-term care considerations.** Some modern life insurance policies include long-term care riders that can provide funds for in-home care, assisted living, or nursing home costs. For pre-retirees who don't have standalone LTC insurance, this is worth exploring.

**Estate planning.** If your estate exceeds the federal estate tax exemption ($13.99M per person in 2026, but subject to change), life insurance placed in an irrevocable trust can provide tax-free funds to pay the estate tax without forcing heirs to sell assets. This is a high-net-worth consideration but increasingly relevant as the exemption declines.

## How to actually do a review

Reviews don't need to be elaborate. Here's the process:

**Step 1: Gather your documents.** Pull out every life insurance policy you own — personal, through work, through any professional association. If you can't find a policy, call the carrier; they'll send you an in-force illustration showing exactly what you have.

**Step 2: Run DIME on your current situation.** Use our [Coverage Needs Calculator](/calculator) to estimate what you actually need today.

**Step 3: Compare.** Is your total coverage (personal + work) within 20% of the DIME number? If yes, you're in reasonable shape. If you're significantly over or under, something needs to change.

**Step 4: Get an independent second opinion.** A free consultation with an independent advisor takes 15 minutes and will tell you:
- Whether your existing policies are priced competitively
- Whether your coverage type still fits your situation
- Whether any conversion options or riders should be exercised while available
- What changes (if any) would improve your plan

**Step 5: Make adjustments.** You may need to add coverage, drop coverage you're overpaying for, exercise a conversion option, or update beneficiaries. None of these takes long once you know what you're doing.

## The one review everyone should do right now

If you haven't looked at your life insurance in 3+ years, you're overdue.

Ask yourself:
- Do I even know how much coverage I have?
- Do I know who the beneficiaries are?
- Has my family changed since I bought the policy?
- Has my income changed by more than 20%?
- Do I still have a mortgage, or is it paid off?
- Has my health changed?

If any of those triggered a "hmm," a 15-minute review is warranted. No cost, no obligation. Just a second set of eyes on a part of your financial life most people set and forget.
