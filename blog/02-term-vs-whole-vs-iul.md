# Term vs. Whole Life vs. IUL: Which Life Insurance Is Right for You?

*By Chris Ihler, Licensed Insurance Advisor · Trust Life Solutions*

---

Life insurance sounds like one product. It isn't. Walk into any conversation with an advisor and you're likely to hear three very different options pitched at you: term life, whole life, and indexed universal life (IUL). Each solves a different problem. Each costs a different amount. And each is perfect for roughly 10% of buyers and wrong for the other 90%.

The problem most consumers face isn't that they chose the wrong product deliberately. It's that the product that got sold to them was the one that paid the biggest commission, not the one that fit their situation. This post explains each type clearly, who each is right for, and how to tell which fits you.

## The 90/9/1 rule (start here)

Before we go deep, the fast answer: **about 90% of families need term life. About 9% need whole life or IUL. About 1% need a combination of multiple types.**

That's not me undermining the industry. It's the honest truth, and any independent advisor will tell you the same. Complexity has a place — but most families have simple needs that term insurance solves cheaply and cleanly.

## Term life insurance

**What it is:** A policy that pays a death benefit if you die within a specified "term" (10, 15, 20, 25, or 30 years) and expires with no payout if you don't. Premiums are locked for the term.

**What it costs:** For a healthy 40-year-old nonsmoker, $500,000 of 20-year term runs roughly $25–$35 per month. A healthy 30-year-old pays closer to $18 per month.

**Who it's right for:**

- **Anyone with a mortgage** they'd want paid off if they died
- **Families with dependents** (spouse, kids) who rely on their income
- **Young couples** starting out with debt and future obligations
- **Business partners** needing buy-sell funding (term policies, business-owned)
- **Anyone needing high coverage cheaply** — term gives you 5–15x more coverage per dollar than permanent options

**Why it works:**

Term insurance is a pure risk-transfer product. You're paying a small, predictable premium to transfer the financial risk of your death to an insurance company. If the low-probability event happens, your family gets a large payout. If it doesn't, the policy expires. You paid for protection. That protection protected.

The critical insight: most of the financial needs that require life insurance have an expiration date. Your mortgage gets paid off. Your kids grow up. Your retirement accounts grow to the point where they can replace your income. By the time you're 60 or 65, you've likely accumulated enough assets that your family doesn't need the death benefit of a life insurance policy to survive. They'll survive on what you've already built.

Term aligns with this reality. You buy protection during the years you need it, you don't pay for it after.

**Why it doesn't fit everyone:**

Term insurance expires. If you need coverage past age 70–75, term is the wrong tool — renewing term at older ages gets extraordinarily expensive, and many term policies can't be renewed past a certain age.

Term also builds no cash value. There's no "investment" component — your premiums pay for the risk pool. Some people don't like the idea of paying premiums for decades with "nothing to show" if they don't die. But remember: you wouldn't complain that your car insurance premiums have "nothing to show" because you didn't total your car. Term works the same way.

### When term is definitely right for you:
- You have dependents and a mortgage
- You're under 55
- You don't already have maxed retirement accounts
- You'll need coverage for a finite period (20–30 years)
- You want the most coverage per dollar of premium

## Whole life insurance

**What it is:** A policy that covers you for your entire life (hence "whole") and builds a cash value component over time. Premiums are higher and fixed for life.

**What it costs:** For a healthy 40-year-old nonsmoker, $500,000 of whole life runs roughly $480–$580 per month — 12–20x the cost of the equivalent term policy.

**How the cash value works:**

Part of every premium goes into a cash account inside the policy. Over time, this account grows at a guaranteed minimum rate (typically 2–4%) plus potential dividends from the insurance company (another 2–4%). You can borrow against the cash value, withdraw it (with tax implications), or surrender the policy for its cash value if you stop wanting coverage.

**Who it's right for:**

- **High-income earners** who've maxed out other tax-advantaged accounts (401k, IRA, HSA) and want another tax-advantaged vehicle
- **People with lifetime insurance needs** — second-to-die policies for estate planning, coverage for a special-needs dependent, or final-expense coverage they want guaranteed to pay regardless of when they die
- **Infinite banking / "be your own banker" strategists** — a niche use case where high-cash-value whole life is used as a personal banking asset
- **Legacy planners** — people who want to leave a guaranteed tax-free payout to heirs or charity at their eventual death
- **Business owners** funding deferred compensation plans or key-person with a permanent component

**Why it works for the right person:**

The cash value growth inside a whole life policy is tax-deferred (you don't pay taxes on growth as it accumulates). Properly structured, you can access that cash through policy loans without triggering taxable events. The death benefit is paid tax-free to beneficiaries. For a high-income earner who's already saving aggressively in other tax-advantaged accounts and wants additional tax-preferenced growth, whole life offers a tool that exists nowhere else in the financial landscape.

**Why it's wrong for most people:**

For the average family, whole life's cost-to-coverage ratio is brutal. A 35-year-old family with $75,000 in income can afford maybe $50/month for life insurance. At $50/month, you get $1,000,000 of term OR $50,000 of whole life. The term coverage is 20x larger. For a family worried about replacing income and paying off a mortgage, 20x the coverage is a no-brainer.

The "forced savings" argument — that whole life's cash value is a hidden savings account — is a weaker argument than it used to be. For most people, maximizing a 401(k) match, contributing to a Roth IRA, and building a brokerage account produces better long-term wealth than whole life's cash value.

**The trap most people fall into:**

Whole life is profitable to sell. An agent earns roughly 60–90% of the first-year premium as commission on a whole life policy — versus 40–60% on a term policy. That's why whole life gets pitched to buyers whose situation doesn't need it.

If an advisor is pushing whole life to a young family with a mortgage and no maxed-out 401(k), get a second opinion. Always.

### When whole life is definitely right for you:
- You earn $200k+ and have maxed out 401(k), IRA, HSA
- You need permanent coverage (legacy, special needs, estate taxes)
- You want a tax-advantaged cash-value vehicle specifically
- You understand and accept the low internal rate of return (typically 3–5% long-term)
- You've been advised by someone who doesn't earn a commission on the recommendation

## Indexed universal life (IUL)

**What it is:** A permanent life insurance policy with a death benefit plus a cash value component tied to a market index (typically the S&P 500). The cash value participates in market gains up to a cap, with a floor of 0% protecting against losses.

**What it costs:** For a healthy 40-year-old nonsmoker, a typical $500,000 IUL runs $200–$400 per month — less than whole life, more than term.

**How it works:**

Premiums are flexible (within IRS minimums and maximums). Part of each premium goes into cash value, which grows based on a selected market index. In a year when the index is up 15%, your cash value might grow at the policy's cap (often 9–11%). In a year when the index is down 20%, your cash value doesn't lose anything — it's floored at 0% (or sometimes 1%).

Over time, you can access cash value through policy loans — tax-free if structured correctly.

**Who it's right for:**

- **High-income earners** (typically $250k+) who've maxed other tax-advantaged accounts
- **People seeking supplemental tax-advantaged retirement income** in addition to 401k and Roth IRA
- **Business owners** funding non-qualified deferred comp or executive bonus plans
- **Sophisticated buyers** who understand the policy structure and will actively manage it

**Why it works for the right person:**

IUL addresses one critique of whole life — the low fixed return — by linking growth to market performance while protecting against market losses. For a high earner looking for tax-advantaged growth beyond 401(k) and IRA limits, a properly structured IUL can produce meaningful tax-free income in retirement.

The structure is genuinely interesting: participation in upside, protection on downside, tax-deferred growth, tax-free access via loans, tax-free death benefit. Few financial products offer all of those features simultaneously.

**Why it's wrong for most people:**

IUL is complex. Participation caps, index crediting methods, policy loans, over-loan protection riders, multi-year guarantees — the number of moving parts creates real risk of underperformance if the policy isn't structured and managed correctly. Bad IULs have resulted in lapsed policies that left owners with huge tax bills and no coverage.

Also: the insurance cost inside an IUL rises as you age. If your cash value doesn't grow fast enough to cover rising insurance costs, the policy erodes. This doesn't happen with whole life (which has level premiums) or with term (which has no cash-value component to erode).

IULs require active management, understanding of complex illustrations, and trust in your advisor's commitment to the long-term structure of the policy. Most people don't have the time, knowledge, or advisor relationship to do this well.

### When IUL is definitely right for you:
- You earn $250k+ with maxed-out traditional retirement accounts
- You understand complex financial products and want to learn the details
- You have an advisor you trust long-term (not someone who sold you once and disappeared)
- You can commit to paying into the policy for 15+ years minimum
- You want market-linked growth with downside protection

## How to choose — a simple decision tree

**Start with term.** Ask yourself:

1. Do I need coverage for the rest of my life (to pay estate taxes, fund legacy, cover special-needs child, etc.)?
   - **No:** Term is the answer. Stop here.
   - **Yes:** Continue to question 2.

2. Have I maxed out my 401(k) match, IRA, and HSA contributions?
   - **No:** Max those first. Term for now; revisit permanent coverage in a few years.
   - **Yes:** Continue to question 3.

3. Am I earning $200k+ and looking for additional tax-advantaged growth?
   - **No:** Term, and invest the difference in a standard brokerage account.
   - **Yes:** Consider whole life or IUL. Get an independent second opinion before buying.

4. Whole life vs IUL: Do I want guaranteed returns and simple structure, or market-linked returns with more moving parts?
   - **Guaranteed, simple:** Whole life.
   - **Market-linked, flexible, complex:** IUL.

Most people never get past question 1. That's fine. Term is a great product when it fits — and it fits most people.

## The red flags of a bad recommendation

You're being pushed toward whole life or IUL when:

- You're under 30, just starting out, with no dependents and modest income
- You have a mortgage and young kids but haven't been shown term as the primary option
- You haven't maxed out 401(k) match, Roth IRA, and HSA
- The advisor hasn't asked about your existing retirement savings
- You're getting a quote for less than $500k of coverage on a permanent policy
- The premium is more than 3% of your gross income
- You weren't shown a term comparison

Any of those signals should trigger an independent second opinion. It's free, it's fast, and it may save you tens of thousands of dollars over time.
