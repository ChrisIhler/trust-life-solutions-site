# Life Insurance With Diabetes: What You Actually Need to Know in 2026

**Target keywords:** life insurance with diabetes, life insurance type 2 diabetes, best life insurance for diabetics
**Reading time:** 7 min
**Target audience:** Diabetic (Type 1 or 2) prospects searching for coverage after hearing "no" elsewhere

If you have diabetes and you've been told you can't get life insurance — or that it'll be absurdly expensive — that advice is probably wrong. Almost every diabetic can qualify for traditional life insurance in 2026. The real question is *which carrier* to apply to, and whether you're getting quoted by an agent who actually knows the landscape.

This post walks you through what actually happens when a diabetic applies for life insurance, what you can expect to pay, and why two applications to the same carrier can come back with wildly different results depending on who submits them.

## The short version

- **Type 2 diabetes, well-controlled on oral meds?** Most applicants qualify for Standard or Standard Plus rates. Monthly premium is often $40–$90 for $500k of 20-year term in your 40s.
- **Type 2 on insulin, A1C under 8?** Substandard (Table 2–4) is typical. Premium is 1.5×–2.5× standard rates.
- **Type 1 diabetes?** You can still get coverage, but Table 4–6 is standard. Prudential is usually your best bet.
- **Uncontrolled diabetes (A1C over 9) or recent diagnosis?** Apply but expect a Rated Table 4–6 or a postponement. Work on control for 6 months and reapply.

The biggest mistake diabetic applicants make: applying to the wrong carrier first. Every carrier sees diabetes differently. One company's "decline" is another's "Standard Plus." Knowing which to apply to is the whole game.

## What the carrier actually looks at

When you apply as a diabetic, underwriters want six pieces of information. Get these together before you apply; it speeds everything up by 2-3 weeks.

1. **A1C history** — especially your last three readings over the past 24 months
2. **Date of diagnosis** — earlier diagnosis is generally better for underwriting than recent
3. **Current treatment** — diet/exercise, oral medications, or insulin
4. **Other cardiovascular risk factors** — high blood pressure, high cholesterol, tobacco use, obesity
5. **Diabetic complications** — neuropathy, retinopathy, kidney issues, any amputations
6. **Primary care doctor's name and last visit date** — they'll pull the records

Carriers weigh these differently. A 50-year-old Type 2 diabetic with A1C of 6.8, no complications, normal BP, and a doctor visit within 12 months looks very different from a 50-year-old with A1C of 8.5, high BP, and no recent PCP visit — even though both are "Type 2 diabetic."

## Which carriers are friendliest to diabetics in 2026

Based on current underwriting appetites (this changes — always verify):

**Best for Type 2 diabetes:**
- **Prudential** — the most flexible underwriter in the market for diabetes. Well-controlled T2 with A1C under 6.5 on orals can sometimes get Standard Plus. Strong appetite for T2 even with insulin if A1C is under 7.5.
- **Banner Life (Legal & General America)** — competitive for T2 on oral meds. Doesn't penalize as heavily for BMI as other carriers, which matters for diabetic applicants.
- **Mutual of Omaha** — reasonable rates and one of the fastest no-exam options up to $500k for controlled T2.

**Best for Type 1 diabetes:**
- **Prudential** — still usually the first stop. Has specific T1 underwriting guidelines and won't bucket T1 applicants with uncontrolled T2.
- **John Hancock Vitality** — if you're willing to wear a fitness tracker and meet wellness goals, their Vitality program gives you ongoing premium reductions.
- **Mutual of Omaha** — simplified-issue products can be a backup for rated cases up to age 75.

**Carriers that are harsher on diabetes:**
- **Northwestern Mutual** and **MassMutual** tend to underwrite diabetes conservatively. You can get coverage, but you're often better served elsewhere on rate.
- **Lemonade** and other fast-issue online products often auto-decline or auto-table diabetics at levels an independent agent could beat.

**If a standard carrier declines:**
- **Mutual of Omaha Living Promise** (final expense / simplified whole life) — $25k–$30k face, a few health questions, no exam
- **Gerber Life** or **AIG Guaranteed Issue** — no health questions at all, $25k max, 2-year graded death benefit

## The three most expensive mistakes diabetic applicants make

**Mistake 1 — Applying to just one carrier.**

Underwriting is not standardized. One carrier's decline is another's approval. If your first agent runs you through just their preferred carrier and you get rated up, you haven't been shopped — you've been tested. Always work with an independent agent who can quote 10+ carriers in parallel.

**Mistake 2 — Letting the agent submit before you've stabilized.**

If your A1C has been creeping up because you recently changed medications, it might be worth waiting 3-6 months to establish a stable reading before applying. A single bad A1C during the underwriting window can drop you a full rate class — that's $300-$600 a year in premium for 20 years.

**Mistake 3 — Not asking about conversion options.**

Term policies with strong conversion riders let you swap to permanent coverage later without a new medical exam. For diabetics, this is huge — if your condition progresses, you can still get permanent coverage at your original health class. Banner OPTerm, Pacific PL Promise, and Penn Mutual's convertible term are particularly strong here.

## The one thing most agents don't tell you

Many diabetic applicants assume the rate they're quoted is the rate they'll get. It isn't. It's a best-case quote based on the information you've given the agent. The actual rate comes back after the carrier reviews your medical records, and it's often a rate class or two worse than quoted.

A good agent accounts for this by quoting you at a conservative rate class upfront (one class below what you'd theoretically qualify for) so you're pleasantly surprised rather than gut-punched when the offer comes back. Be suspicious of an agent who quotes you the absolute best-case number without asking about recent A1Cs, complications, and BMI first.
